## Reading the numbers

Blood pressure is written as systolic over diastolic, in millimetres of mercury.

| Category | Systolic | Diastolic |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | below 120 | and below 80 |
| Elevated | 120 to 129 | and below 80 |
| Stage 1 hypertension | 130 to 139 | or 80 to 89 |
| Stage 2 hypertension | 140 or above | or 90 or above |
| Hypertensive crisis | above 180 | or above 120 |

WHO and most Indian programmes treat a sustained reading of **140/90 or above** as the practical threshold for diagnosing hypertension and starting treatment. A diagnosis needs readings on more than one occasion, not a single high value at a clinic.

Hypertension usually causes no symptoms at all. That is exactly why it damages the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes silently for years. Headache, nosebleeds and dizziness are **not** reliable signs of high blood pressure, and their absence does not mean the pressure is fine.

## Measuring blood pressure correctly at home

Poor technique is the most common cause of a misleading reading:

- Sit quietly for five minutes first. No tea, coffee, tobacco or exercise for 30 minutes before.
- Sit with back supported, feet flat on the floor, legs uncrossed.
- Rest the arm on a table so the cuff is at heart level.
- Use the correct cuff size on a bare upper arm, not over a sleeve.
- Do not talk during the measurement.
- Take two or three readings a minute apart and record the average, along with the date and time.

Bring the logbook or device to appointments. A record of home readings is far more useful to a doctor than a single clinic value.

## Hypertensive emergency

A reading above **180/120** needs attention. Call **112** or go to an emergency department immediately if a very high reading comes with any of:

- Chest pain or severe breathlessness
- Severe headache with confusion, blurred vision or difficulty speaking
- Weakness or numbness on one side of the body
- Seizures
- Blood in the urine, or a sharp fall in urine output
- Severe nosebleed that will not stop

If the reading is very high but there are no symptoms, rest quietly for five minutes and repeat the measurement, then contact your doctor the same day. Do not take an extra dose of your medicine on your own, and do not take a neighbour or relative medicine. Rapidly dropping blood pressure can itself cause a stroke.

## Lowering blood pressure without medicines

These steps work, and they improve the effect of medicines rather than replacing them:

- **Salt is the biggest lever in the Indian diet.** WHO advises less than 5 grams of salt a day, about one level teaspoon from all sources. Most of it comes from pickles, papad, namkeen, packaged snacks, sauces, processed meats and restaurant food, not from the salt shaker.
- Increase potassium from fruit, vegetables and pulses unless your doctor has restricted potassium because of kidney disease.
- Lose weight if the waist is above 90 cm for men or 80 cm for women. Even 5 kg makes a measurable difference.
- 150 minutes a week of brisk walking, cycling or swimming.
- Limit alcohol, and stop tobacco entirely.
- Treat sleep apnoea if you snore heavily and feel sleepy through the day.
- Manage stress with sleep, routine and breathing practice. This helps, but it does not substitute for treatment when the pressure is high.

## Taking medicines reliably

Blood pressure medicines control, rather than cure. Stopping them when readings normalise is the most common reason control is lost, and rebound rises can be dangerous.

- Take them at the same time each day, linked to a fixed daily habit.
- If you get side effects such as a dry cough, ankle swelling or dizziness on standing, report them. There are many alternative drug classes, and switching is routine.
- Get a repeat prescription before the strip runs out. Free medicines are available at government facilities under national programmes in many districts.
- Tell any doctor treating you about all medicines you take, including painkillers. Regular NSAID use raises blood pressure and can blunt the effect of your treatment.
