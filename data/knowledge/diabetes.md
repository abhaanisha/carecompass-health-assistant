## Understanding the numbers

Diabetes is diagnosed by a clinician using blood tests, not by symptoms alone. The commonly used thresholds are:

| Test | Normal | Prediabetes | Diabetes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fasting plasma glucose | below 100 mg/dL | 100 to 125 mg/dL | 126 mg/dL or above |
| 2-hour post-glucose | below 140 mg/dL | 140 to 199 mg/dL | 200 mg/dL or above |
| HbA1c | below 5.7 percent | 5.7 to 6.4 percent | 6.5 percent or above |

A single abnormal reading is not a diagnosis. It needs confirmation. HbA1c reflects average glucose over roughly the previous three months and is the usual measure of long-term control. Individual targets differ: older adults and people prone to low sugars are often given a deliberately looser target.

Common early symptoms are excessive thirst, frequent urination including at night, unexplained weight loss, persistent tiredness, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds and recurrent skin or genital infections. Many people have none of these at diagnosis, which is why screening matters for anyone over 30 with a family history, raised waist circumference, high blood pressure, or a history of gestational diabetes.

## Low blood sugar, hypoglycaemia, is the acute emergency

Low blood sugar, usually below 70 mg/dL, can develop within minutes in people taking insulin or sulfonylurea tablets, especially after a missed meal, extra exertion, alcohol, or an accidental double dose.

Warning signs: shakiness, sweating, palpitations, intense hunger, anxiety, irritability, confusion, slurred speech, blurred vision, and in severe cases seizures or unconsciousness.

If the person is **awake and able to swallow**, the standard first-aid approach is the rule of 15: give about 15 grams of fast-acting sugar, for example three to four teaspoons of sugar or glucose powder in water, half a cup of regular fruit juice or a regular soft drink, or three to four glucose tablets. Wait 15 minutes and recheck. Repeat once if symptoms persist, then follow with a snack containing starch and protein.

Do not use chocolate, ice cream or milk-based sweets as the first treatment, because fat slows sugar absorption.

If the person is **drowsy, unconscious or fitting, do not put anything in their mouth.** Call **112** immediately, place them on their side, and give injectable glucagon if it has been prescribed and you know how to use it.

Anyone who has had a severe hypo needs their treatment reviewed by their doctor, not just a one-off correction.

## High blood sugar and diabetic ketoacidosis

Very high blood sugar over days causes intense thirst, heavy urination, weakness and weight loss. This becomes a medical emergency when ketones build up. Go to hospital immediately if there is:

- Vomiting that will not stop, with abdominal pain
- Deep, rapid, sighing breathing
- Breath that smells sweet or of nail polish remover
- Drowsiness or confusion
- Blood sugar that stays very high with moderate or large ketones on a urine or blood ketone test

Diabetic ketoacidosis is most common in type 1 diabetes, but it happens in type 2 as well, and is frequently triggered by an infection or by stopping insulin. **Never stop insulin during an illness**, even when eating less. Sick-day rules are to keep taking insulin, check sugars more often, check ketones, and keep fluids going.

## Daily care that prevents complications

- Take medicines at the times prescribed. If cost or side effects are a problem, tell your doctor rather than stopping silently.
- Build meals around whole grains, pulses, vegetables and adequate protein. Reduce refined flour, white rice portions, fried snacks, sugary drinks and sweets. It is portion size and total carbohydrate load that matter more than avoiding any one food.
- Aim for 30 minutes of walking or equivalent activity most days, plus resistance exercise twice a week if possible.
- Inspect the feet every day for cuts, blisters, colour change or numbness. Never walk barefoot, even indoors. A foot ulcer in a person with diabetes is a same-day medical problem, not something to treat at home.
- Have an eye examination with pupil dilation at least once a year. Diabetic retinopathy causes no symptoms until damage is advanced.
- Have kidney function and urine protein checked annually, and keep blood pressure controlled, since it drives kidney and eye damage as much as sugar does.
- Stop tobacco completely, and get vaccinated as advised, since infections are both more likely and more severe.
