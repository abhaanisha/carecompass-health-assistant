## This assistant does not prescribe

Choosing a medicine, its strength, its frequency and its duration depends on your weight, age, kidney and liver function, pregnancy status, other conditions and every other medicine you take. That decision belongs to a qualified clinician or pharmacist who can ask those questions. What follows is general safety information, not a prescription.

Always follow the dose printed on the pack or given by your doctor, and never exceed it.

## Paracetamol, the most common accidental overdose

Paracetamol, also sold as acetaminophen, is safe at recommended doses and dangerous above them. The margin is narrower than most people assume.

- **Never take two products containing paracetamol at the same time.** It is hidden inside many combination cold, flu, headache and body-ache preparations sold under brand names that do not mention it. Read the ingredients on every pack.
- Do not exceed the maximum daily amount stated on the pack, and keep at least four hours between doses.
- People with liver disease, those who drink alcohol regularly, those who are underweight, and those who have eaten very little need lower amounts. Ask a pharmacist.
- Children are dosed by weight, not by age or by splitting adult tablets. Use the syringe or cup supplied with the bottle, not a kitchen spoon.
- Overdose causes no symptoms at first. Liver damage becomes apparent after a day or more, by which time the antidote works less well. **If an overdose is even suspected, go to hospital immediately** and call the poison helpline **1800-116-117**. Do not wait for symptoms.

## NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, diclofenac and aspirin

These relieve pain and inflammation but carry real risks:

- Take with or after food, and use the lowest amount for the shortest time.
- Avoid or use only under medical advice if you have a stomach ulcer or reflux, kidney disease, heart failure, uncontrolled blood pressure, or asthma worsened by aspirin.
- Avoid in **dengue or any undiagnosed fever with a bleeding tendency**, since they increase bleeding risk. Paracetamol is preferred there.
- Avoid in the third trimester of pregnancy.
- Do not combine two different NSAIDs. Many pain balms, sprays and combination tablets contain one.
- Regular use raises blood pressure and can reduce the effect of blood pressure medicines.
- Aspirin should not be given to children and teenagers with a viral illness because of the risk of Reye syndrome.

## Antibiotics and antimicrobial resistance

Antibiotics kill bacteria. They do nothing for colds, flu, most sore throats, most coughs and most cases of diarrhoea, all of which are usually viral.

India carries one of the world highest burdens of antimicrobial resistance, driven largely by antibiotics bought without prescription and courses left unfinished. Practical rules:

- Do not buy antibiotics over the counter, and do not take leftovers from a previous illness or from a family member.
- If a doctor prescribes one, take it exactly as directed and complete the course they specified. Stopping when you feel better leaves the hardiest bacteria alive.
- Never pressure a doctor for an antibiotic. A prescription that is not needed carries side effects, disturbs your gut bacteria, and makes future infections harder to treat.
- Report allergies before every prescription, and know the difference between a true allergy such as rash, swelling or breathlessness and a side effect such as nausea.

## Interactions worth knowing about

Tell every doctor and pharmacist about all your medicines, including ayurvedic, homeopathic, herbal and over-the-counter products, since interactions are common and often overlooked.

| Combination | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Warfarin with NSAIDs, aspirin or some antibiotics | Substantially raises bleeding risk |
| ACE inhibitors or ARBs with NSAIDs and diuretics | Can precipitate acute kidney injury |
| Metformin around a contrast CT scan | Usually paused around the scan on medical advice |
| Sedatives, sleeping tablets or opioids with alcohol | Suppresses breathing, can be fatal |
| Statins with certain antifungals, antibiotics or grapefruit juice | Raises the risk of muscle damage |
| St John wort with antidepressants, contraceptives or antiretrovirals | Reduces their effect and can cause serotonin toxicity |
| Two combination cold preparations together | Silent duplication of paracetamol or of decongestants |

## Storing and disposing of medicines safely

Keep medicines in a cool, dry place out of the reach of children, and keep the original pack with the expiry date and instructions. Heat and humidity degrade many drugs, so a kitchen shelf above the stove or a bathroom cabinet is a poor choice. Do not use medicines past their expiry date, and do not flush them or throw strips into open waste where children or animals can reach them. Return unused medicines to a pharmacy take-back point where one exists.

Keep a single written list of everything you take, with names and strengths, and carry it to every appointment and hospital visit. For anyone on more than three regular medicines, that list prevents more harm than any individual precaution.
