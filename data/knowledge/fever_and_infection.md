## What counts as a fever

A fever is usually defined as a body temperature of **38 C (100.4 F) or higher**. Most fevers are caused by infections the body clears on its own within a few days.

How high the fever goes is a poor guide to how serious the illness is. How the person looks and behaves matters much more than the number on the thermometer. A child who is drinking, alert and playing between temperature spikes is usually less worrying than a child who is limp and refusing fluids at a lower temperature.

Measure temperature with a digital thermometer in the mouth or armpit. Add roughly 0.5 C to an armpit reading. Avoid measuring straight after a hot drink or a bath.

## Self-care for an uncomplicated fever

- Drink fluids often: water, ORS, coconut water, thin dal, buttermilk or soup. Adequate urine output that is pale in colour is a good sign.
- Rest. Stay off work or school while feverish, both to recover and to avoid spreading infection.
- Dress lightly and keep the room ventilated. Do not wrap a feverish person in heavy blankets.
- Paracetamol may be used for comfort as directed on the pack. It brings the temperature down but does not treat the cause.
- Sponging with lukewarm water can help comfort. Never use ice water or alcohol rubs.
- Do not take antibiotics for a fever without a doctor assessment. Most fevers are viral and antibiotics will not help.

## Red flags with fever that need urgent care

Seek medical care the same day, or go to an emergency department, if a fever comes with any of these:

- A stiff neck, severe headache, dislike of bright light, or a rash that does not fade when pressed with a glass, which can indicate meningitis
- Difficulty breathing, rapid breathing, or chest pain
- Confusion, extreme drowsiness, or a first-time seizure
- Persistent vomiting so that fluids cannot be kept down, or no urine for 8 hours or more
- Fever lasting more than 3 days without improvement, or a fever that settles and then returns
- Fever in someone who is pregnant, is on cancer treatment or steroids, has had an organ transplant, has sickle cell disease, or has had their spleen removed
- Fever in an infant under 3 months of age, which always needs same-day medical review
- Fever with severe pain in one area, for example flank pain with burning urination, or a hot swollen joint

## Dengue warning signs that need hospital care

Dengue is common in India during and after the monsoon. It typically causes high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain, and sometimes a rash.

The dangerous phase is usually **day 3 to day 7 of illness, often as the fever starts to fall**. Go to hospital immediately if any of these appear:

- Severe or continuous abdominal pain
- Persistent vomiting
- Bleeding from the gums or nose, blood in vomit or stool, or black tarry stools
- Unusual bleeding under the skin, or new bruising
- Restlessness, irritability, or sudden lethargy and cold clammy skin
- Rapid breathing
- A sharp fall in urine output

**Do not take ibuprofen, aspirin or other NSAID painkillers if dengue is suspected**, because they increase bleeding risk. Paracetamol is the preferred fever medicine in suspected dengue. Keep fluid intake up and get a platelet count done as advised by a doctor.

## Malaria, typhoid and other causes to consider

In many parts of India, a fever with chills and sweating should prompt a malaria test, especially after travel to a high-transmission area. Malaria can become severe quickly, and a fever with drowsiness, jaundice, dark urine or breathlessness is an emergency.

A fever that builds gradually over a week with abdominal discomfort and altered bowel habit may suggest typhoid. A fever with burning urination suggests a urinary infection. A fever with a productive cough for more than two weeks, weight loss and night sweats should always be evaluated for tuberculosis.

Only a clinician with access to examination and tests can distinguish between these. The role of self-care is to stay hydrated, control discomfort, and recognise when to seek help.
