## Burns and scalds

1. Move the person away from the source of heat and stop the burning.
2. **Cool the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes.** This works up to three hours after the injury and is the single most effective step.
3. Remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the burn before swelling starts, unless the fabric is stuck to the skin.
4. Cover loosely with a clean, non-fluffy cloth or cling film laid over, not wrapped around.
5. Keep the person warm overall, since prolonged cooling can drop body temperature, especially in children.

**Never** apply ice, toothpaste, butter, ghee, oil, turmeric, ink, mud or any home remedy. They trap heat, introduce infection, and make the wound harder to assess.

Go to hospital for burns that are larger than the palm of the person hand, burns on the face, hands, feet, genitals or across a joint, any burn that looks white, leathery or charred, any burn that is painless because nerve endings are destroyed, chemical and electrical burns, and any burn in an infant or older adult. Suspect airway burns and call **112** if there is soot around the nose or mouth, a hoarse voice, or singed nasal hair.

## Bleeding wounds

Apply firm, direct pressure over the wound with a clean cloth and keep pressing without lifting to check. Add more cloth on top if it soaks through. Raise the injured limb above heart level if there is no suspected fracture. Lay the person down if they feel faint.

Do not use a tourniquet unless bleeding is catastrophic, other measures have failed, and you know how to apply one. Do not remove an embedded object, since it may be plugging the vessel; pad around it instead.

Seek medical care for wounds that will not stop bleeding after 10 minutes of firm pressure, deep or gaping wounds that may need stitches, wounds over a joint, animal or human bites, puncture wounds from dirty or rusty objects, and any wound with embedded dirt that will not rinse out. Ask about **tetanus** cover for any dirty or puncture wound if your last booster was more than five years ago.

## Choking

If the person can cough forcefully, encourage them to keep coughing. Do not intervene.

If they cannot speak, cough or breathe:

1. Give up to five sharp back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, with the person leaning forward.
2. If that fails, give up to five abdominal thrusts, standing behind them with a fist above the navel and pulling sharply inwards and upwards.
3. Alternate five and five, and call **112** if the obstruction does not clear.
4. If they become unresponsive, lower them to the ground and begin chest compressions.

For an infant under one year, use five back blows followed by five chest thrusts with two fingers on the breastbone. **Never use abdominal thrusts on an infant.** Anyone who has received abdominal thrusts should be checked by a doctor afterwards.

## Snakebite

Snakebite kills tens of thousands of people in India each year, and most deaths are preventable with early hospital treatment.

**Do:** keep the person calm and still, since movement spreads venom. Immobilise the bitten limb with a splint and keep it at or below heart level. Remove rings, bangles and anything tight. Note the time of the bite and take a photograph of the snake only if it is safe from a distance. Get to a hospital with antivenom as fast as possible, by vehicle, not on foot.

**Do not:** apply a tourniquet, cut the wound, suck out venom, apply ice or chemicals, give alcohol, or waste time with traditional healers and snake stones. Do not try to catch or kill the snake.

Watch for drooping eyelids, difficulty speaking or swallowing, breathlessness, bleeding from the gums, or dark urine. These mean serious envenomation and need immediate hospital care. Antivenom is available free at most government hospitals in India.

## Heat exhaustion and heat stroke

Heat exhaustion causes heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, cramps, headache and a fast pulse. Move the person to shade or a cool room, lie them down and raise the legs, loosen clothing, cool the skin with wet cloths and a fan, and give ORS or lightly salted water in sips. They should improve within 30 minutes.

**Heat stroke is a medical emergency.** Suspect it when there is confusion, agitation, slurred speech, seizures or unconsciousness, with very hot skin that may be dry or wet, and a core temperature around 40 C or above. Call **112**, move the person to the coolest place available, remove outer clothing, and cool aggressively with cold water over the body, wet sheets and fanning, or ice packs at the neck, armpits and groin. Do not give fluids by mouth to someone who is confused or drowsy.

Prevention during Indian summers: drink water before you feel thirsty, avoid outdoor work between 12 and 4, wear loose light clothing, and never leave a child or older adult in a parked vehicle.

## Suspected fractures and head injury

Do not try to straighten a deformed limb. Support it in the position found, immobilise the joints above and below, and apply cold packs over cloth for pain and swelling.

After a head injury, get emergency care for loss of consciousness of any duration, repeated vomiting, worsening headache, drowsiness or confusion, a fit, clear fluid or blood from the nose or ear, unequal pupils, weakness or numbness, or any head injury in a person taking blood thinners. Observe closely for 24 hours even when the initial injury seems minor, and wake the person periodically overnight if the injury was significant.
