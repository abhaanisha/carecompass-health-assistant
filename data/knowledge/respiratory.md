## Common cold and cough, what to expect

Most coughs and colds are viral. A cough often peaks in the first few days and can linger for **two to three weeks** even after the infection has cleared. That slow tail is normal and is not a reason for antibiotics.

Helpful self-care:

- Warm fluids, steam inhalation, and honey in warm water for adults and children over one year. Never give honey to an infant under one year.
- Salt water gargles for a sore throat.
- Sleep propped up on an extra pillow if the cough is worse at night.
- Stop smoking and avoid smoke, dust and strong fumes, which prolong a cough.
- Paracetamol as directed on the pack for aches or sore throat pain.

Cough syrups have limited proven benefit. Combination cough and cold syrups should not be given to young children.

## Red flags, breathing problems that need urgent care

Call **112** or go to an emergency department if there is:

- Severe breathlessness, or breathing so fast that the person cannot complete a sentence
- Blue or grey lips, tongue or fingertips
- Chest pain with breathlessness
- Noisy breathing with drooling and difficulty swallowing, or a sudden inability to speak
- A child who is grunting, whose chest is sucking in between or below the ribs, or whose nostrils flare with each breath
- Confusion or extreme drowsiness with a chest infection
- Coughing up blood

Seek a same-day appointment for: a cough lasting more than three weeks, a fever that returns after improving, wheeze that is new in an adult, or a cough with unexplained weight loss or night sweats.

## A cough lasting more than two weeks

In India, a cough that has lasted **more than two weeks**, especially with fever, night sweats, loss of appetite or weight loss, should be evaluated for **tuberculosis**. Sputum testing and chest imaging are available free of cost at government health facilities under the National TB Elimination Programme. TB is curable, and treatment is free, but it needs the full course to be completed.

Other causes of a long cough include asthma, post-nasal drip from allergies or sinusitis, acid reflux, and side effects from some blood pressure medicines. These need a clinician to sort out.

## Asthma and recognising an attack

Asthma causes wheeze, cough, chest tightness and breathlessness, often worse at night, in cold air, during exercise, or around dust, smoke and pollen.

Signs an attack is becoming dangerous:

- The reliever inhaler is not helping, or is needed again within four hours
- Too breathless to speak, eat or sleep
- Breathing is getting faster and it feels like the breath cannot be caught
- Lips or fingertips turning blue
- A child who has gone quiet and still after a period of distress

These are emergencies. Call **112**, sit the person upright, and help them take their reliever inhaler, ideally through a spacer.

People with asthma should have a written action plan from their doctor, use a spacer with a metered dose inhaler, and understand the difference between a **preventer** inhaler taken daily and a **reliever** inhaler taken for symptoms. Frequent reliever use is a sign the condition is not well controlled and needs review. It is not a reason to simply buy more inhalers.

## Air quality and respiratory health

Across much of northern India, outdoor air quality worsens sharply in winter. On high-pollution days, people with asthma, COPD, heart disease, and young children benefit from limiting outdoor exertion, keeping windows shut during peak pollution hours, and using a well-fitting N95 mask outdoors. Indoor smoke from solid cooking fuel is an equally important and often overlooked cause of chronic lung disease.
