## Chest pain, when it is an emergency

Call **112** or **108** immediately for chest pain that:

- Feels like pressure, heaviness, tightness or squeezing in the centre or left of the chest
- Lasts more than a few minutes, or goes away and comes back
- Spreads to the arm, shoulder, jaw, neck, back or upper abdomen
- Comes with sweating, nausea, vomiting, light-headedness or a sense of doom
- Comes with breathlessness
- Starts during exertion and eases with rest, which is a warning pattern even if it settles

Do not wait to see if it passes. Do not drive yourself. Time is heart muscle: treatment given in the first hour saves the most tissue.

South Asians develop coronary artery disease around a decade earlier on average than many other populations, so a young age does not rule out a heart attack.

## Atypical presentations that are easy to miss

Women, older adults and people with diabetes often do not have classic crushing chest pain. In these groups a heart attack may show up as:

- Unusual fatigue or sudden weakness over hours or days
- Breathlessness without chest pain
- Indigestion-like discomfort, nausea or vomiting
- Pain confined to the jaw, upper back, or between the shoulder blades
- Sudden cold sweat with a feeling of anxiety

Nerve damage from long-standing diabetes can blunt pain entirely, producing a silent heart attack. Any new, unexplained breathlessness or collapse in someone with diabetes deserves urgent assessment.

## While waiting for the ambulance with suspected heart attack

- Help the person sit down, leaning back, with knees bent, and loosen tight clothing.
- If the person is conscious, not allergic to aspirin, and has no bleeding disorder or active bleeding, emergency services often advise chewing a single adult aspirin tablet. Follow the instruction of the emergency operator rather than acting on your own.
- If they carry a prescribed nitrate spray or tablet for angina, help them use it as prescribed.
- If they become unresponsive and are not breathing normally, start chest compressions and send someone for an AED if one is available nearby.

## Stroke, act FAST

A stroke is a blocked or bleeding blood vessel in the brain. Use the **FAST** check:

- **F, Face:** ask the person to smile. Has one side of the face drooped?
- **A, Arms:** ask them to raise both arms. Does one drift down?
- **S, Speech:** ask them to repeat a simple sentence. Is speech slurred or muddled?
- **T, Time:** if any of these are present, call **112** immediately and note the exact time symptoms began.

Other sudden stroke symptoms include loss of vision in one or both eyes, severe unexplained headache described as the worst ever, sudden loss of balance with vomiting, and sudden numbness on one side of the body.

Clot-busting treatment and clot retrieval only work inside a narrow time window, usually measured in hours from symptom onset. The time the person was **last seen well** is the single most useful piece of information you can give the hospital. Do not give food, water or medicines by mouth, because swallowing is often unsafe after a stroke.

## Palpitations and when to worry

A brief awareness of the heartbeat after caffeine, exercise, stress or a poor night of sleep is common and usually harmless. Seek urgent care if palpitations come with fainting or near-fainting, chest pain, severe breathlessness, or if the heartbeat is fast and irregular and does not settle. An irregular pulse that is new should be reviewed by a doctor, because atrial fibrillation substantially raises stroke risk and is treatable.

## Reducing cardiovascular risk

The main modifiable risks are tobacco in any form, high blood pressure, high blood sugar, abnormal cholesterol, abdominal obesity, physical inactivity, and a diet high in salt, sugar and trans fats. Stopping tobacco produces measurable benefit within weeks. At least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, cooking with less salt and oil, and having blood pressure, sugar and lipids checked periodically after the age of 30 are the highest-yield steps for most adults.
