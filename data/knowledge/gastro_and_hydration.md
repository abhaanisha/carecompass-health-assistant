## Diarrhoea and vomiting, the main risk is dehydration

Most acute diarrhoea and vomiting is caused by an infection and settles within a few days. The danger is not the loose stools themselves but the loss of water and salts. Children under five and older adults become dehydrated fastest.

The core treatment is **oral rehydration solution (ORS)** plus continued feeding:

- Dissolve one ORS sachet in exactly **one litre of clean drinking water**. Do not guess proportions, and do not add extra sugar or salt to the mixture.
- Give small sips frequently rather than large amounts at once, especially if there is vomiting. A spoonful every one to two minutes is often better tolerated.
- Discard prepared ORS after 24 hours.
- Keep eating. Continue breastfeeding for infants. Bland, starchy foods such as rice, khichdi, banana, curd and toast are well tolerated.
- Government guidelines recommend **zinc supplementation alongside ORS for children with diarrhoea**, typically for 14 days. Ask a pharmacist, doctor or ASHA worker for the correct amount for your child age.
- Wash hands with soap after using the toilet and before handling food. This is the single most effective way to stop it spreading in a household.

Avoid anti-diarrhoeal medicines such as loperamide in children, and in anyone with blood in the stool or a high fever, because they can trap the infection. Antibiotics are not needed for most cases and should only be taken if a doctor prescribes them.

## Recognising dehydration

Mild to moderate dehydration causes thirst, dry mouth, reduced urine, darker urine and tiredness.

**Severe dehydration is an emergency.** Go to hospital immediately if there is:

- No urine passed for 8 hours or more, or no wet nappy for 6 hours in an infant
- Sunken eyes, or a sunken soft spot on an infant head
- Skin that stays pinched when gently lifted
- Drowsiness, floppiness, or difficulty waking
- Cold, mottled hands and feet with a fast, weak pulse
- Inability to keep any fluid down despite repeated small sips
- Dizziness or fainting on standing

## Red flags with abdominal pain

Get emergency care for abdominal pain that is:

- Sudden, severe, and unlike anything experienced before
- Accompanied by a rigid, board-like abdomen that is tender to the lightest touch
- Associated with vomiting blood, or passing black tarry stools or fresh blood
- Present with a fever and pain that has moved to the lower right abdomen and worsens on movement, a pattern that can indicate appendicitis
- In a pregnant person, or in someone who could be pregnant and has missed a period, because an ectopic pregnancy is life-threatening
- Accompanied by inability to pass stool or wind, with a swollen abdomen and vomiting
- In a person over 50 with new, persistent pain, or with unintended weight loss

Pain with burning urination and flank pain suggests a kidney infection and needs same-day care. Severe pain in waves from the flank to the groin may be a kidney stone.

## Food and water safety

Most acute gastroenteritis in India is preventable. Drink water that has been boiled, filtered or is from a sealed bottle. Eat food that is freshly cooked and served hot. Be cautious with cut fruit, salads washed in untreated water, ice from unknown sources, and food that has sat at room temperature. Reheat leftovers thoroughly rather than warming them lightly.

If several people who ate the same meal fall ill within hours, food poisoning is likely. Most cases resolve with rehydration, but severe cases, and any case with neurological symptoms such as double vision or weakness, need urgent medical attention.
